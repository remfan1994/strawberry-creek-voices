As morning widens through the creek corridor, direct light begins to distinguish surfaces that the first gray had revealed only by outline. The upper faces of shallow stones receive warmth before the deeper channel does. Thin water passing over them brightens and changes temperature more quickly than the current moving beneath the shaded bank. The difference remains local. Every small ridge folds part of the warmed surface downward, while cooler water rises around the stone edges. Reflections lengthen, contract, and break as these exchanges continue. The creek does not divide into separate warm and cool regions. It carries both conditions through the same moving structure. Attention settles where sunlight, stone, and water meet without any one of them becoming the sole cause of what appears. Sunlight reached a line of flat stones near the eastern margin of the creek. Their upper surfaces had stood partly above the water since the level lowered, leaving a dark boundary where wet stone met the lighter area exposed to air. The thinnest water warmed first. It passed over the lower edges of the stones in clear sheets, carrying narrow bands of brightness downstream. Beneath those bands, the deeper current retained the cooler condition of the night. The difference appeared through movement rather than color. Above the warmed stone, the surface showed a slight shimmer. Beside it, shaded water remained smoother and darker. Where the two met, small ridges folded together and continued toward the center of the channel. No fixed boundary remained between them. Water that crossed the sunlit edge entered shade a short distance downstream. Cooler water moved upward around the sides of submerged stones and became part of the illuminated surface. Each condition altered the other without eliminating it. A shallow depression in one stone retained a thin pool separated from the main current by only a low mineral ridge. The water within it reflected a small opening in the canopy. When a surface ripple reached the ridge, a narrow exchange passed over it, renewing the pool without emptying it. Near the bank, moss and fine roots remained outside the direct light. Their surfaces were still dark with retained moisture. The exposed stone warmed beside them, while the creek continued carrying cooler water along their lower edges. The sunlight revealed change at different rates. Stone, surface, depth, and shade entered morning together without becoming uniform. The current passed beyond the brightened edge with both warmth and coolness distributed through it. The exposed stones continue to warm, and the moisture on their upper surfaces begins to withdraw into thinner films. Beside them, the shaded bank remains dark. A low margin of moss extends above the visible waterline, yet its underside holds moisture after the adjacent stone has begun to dry. The source is not a falling drop or a rise in the creek. Water moves through smaller spaces within the bank. Fine soil pores, root surfaces, and fragments of softened bark provide connected passages through which moisture can travel above the level of open water. Attention moves from the bright exchange over stone to this slower upward movement. The creek continues downstream, while part of its nearness is carried into the bank against the simple direction of descent. A narrow shelf of moss followed the bank several centimeters above the creek. Its upper tips had begun to lighten where filtered morning air reached them, but the lower growth remained dark and saturated. No visible stream connected the moss to the channel. Moisture reached it through fine spaces in the soil beneath. Water held between small grains rose along their surfaces and around the root fibers crossing through them. The movement was too slow to form droplets, yet sufficient to maintain a continuous dark band beneath the moss. Fragments of bark lay partly incorporated into the bank. Their softened layers retained water differently from the mineral soil around them. Fine roots passed between the fragments, preserving narrow openings while binding the material together. Near the lower edge, one moss filament bent beneath a bead of moisture. The bead enlarged until it touched the damp soil below, joining the two surfaces without falling. Its boundary disappeared as the water spread back into the shaded material. Higher on the bank, wider pores between gravel grains interrupted the upward movement. Those areas dried sooner. Where the soil was finer, moisture reached farther above the channel and remained longer beneath the moss. The pattern followed structure rather than a uniform height. Darker regions rose beside roots, narrowed across coarse gravel, and widened again where organic material held water close to the surface. Sunlight moved across the creek below, but little reached this margin. The moss remained connected to the channel through a movement that required neither direct immersion nor visible flow. Water descended through the creek and rose within the bank at the same time. The two directions belonged to one continuous distribution of moisture. The capillary-darkened soil follows the fine roots toward a larger alder root entering the creek. Around its base, the bank is neither sealed nor loose. Openings remain between wood, sediment, and gravel, allowing water to pass while the root network holds the margin in place. Morning light reaches these openings from the channel side. It enters narrow spaces beneath the root arch, crosses shallow water, and settles briefly on the sediment behind it. The same structure that supports the bank also permits light, current, and moisture to move through. What appeared in Anchor 74 as hidden upward continuity becomes visible here as permeability. Holding and passage are not opposing functions. They operate together within the same root-bound edge. Several alder roots entered the creek at different angles. One descended directly into the bank. Another curved outward before returning beneath the water. Between them, small openings remained where the current could pass. Morning light entered one of these openings and reached the shallow sediment behind the roots. The illuminated area was narrow, bordered by dark wood above and fine gravel below. As the surface moved, the light contracted and widened without leaving the opening. Water divided around the roots. A thin portion passed beneath the lowest arch, slowed by contact with sediment, and rejoined the main current beyond the root tip. Another portion moved along the exposed outer surface where the channel was deeper. Fine material had gathered in the protected spaces behind the roots. Coarse sand rested nearest the current, while smaller particles settled farther into the bank. Fragments of softened leaf and bark lay among them, gradually entering the darker soil. The roots did not prevent exchange. Moisture moved through the bank behind them. Water passed between them. Light crossed the openings they preserved. Their support depended partly upon the absence of complete closure. A faint surface reflection moved across the upper root. It briefly concealed the grain of the wood, then passed downstream and revealed it again. Beneath the reflection, the root remained in contact with water, air, sediment, and neighboring roots at once. Nothing in the structure acted alone. The bank held because many relations remained intact: root tension, soil cohesion, gravel drainage, and the moderated pressure of the current. Light continued through the openings until the changing angle carried it elsewhere. The passages remained after illumination withdrew. Beyond the alder roots, the current enters a calm margin protected from the faster center of the channel. Reflections from the canopy spread across the surface with few interruptions. The openings beneath the roots remain visible as darker intervals along the bank. A water strider moves from the shadow of an overhanging sedge onto the open surface. Its points of contact press shallow depressions into the water without breaking through it. Each depression carries a small reflected fragment of sky around its edge. The sequence rises from the structural openness of the bank to a living presence supported by the surface itself. Passage continues, but now through contact so light that the water closes behind it almost immediately. A water strider rested on the shaded surface beyond the alder roots. Each point of contact formed a shallow depression, visible as a dark oval surrounded by a thin line of reflected light. The insect remained still while the marginal current moved beneath it. Its position changed only slightly in relation to the bank. Small adjustments of the legs returned it to the quieter water whenever the current carried it toward the center. When it moved, the rearward pressure of its legs formed paired ripples. The body traveled forward while the ripples widened behind it, crossing the reflected alder leaves and fading before they reached the opposite margin. The surface did not remain marked. Each depression closed as soon as a leg lifted. The water returned to continuity without retaining an outline of the contact. A droplet fell from an overhanging leaf several centimeters away. Its circular ripple reached the strider after widening across the shaded surface. The insect rose and settled with the passing wave, then changed its angle toward a narrow patch of calmer water beside the root. Sunlight touched the outer edge of one supporting leg and made the surrounding surface depression briefly clearer. The contact appeared precise: sufficient to distribute weight, slight enough to preserve the surface beneath it. The water strider crossed the calm margin in three short movements. It paused between them, responding to current, ripple, and the changing position of the reflected canopy. At the far edge, it moved beneath a sedge blade and became difficult to distinguish from the bank shadow. The current continued through the space it had crossed. Only the last paired ripples remained, and those soon joined the larger movement of the creek. The final ripples from the water strider travel toward the center of the channel. Most lengthen evenly with the current, but one segment changes shape above the downstream foot of the gravel shelf. Its outer curve slows while the adjacent surface remains in motion. Below, cooler water is returning from the spaces within the creek bed. The upwelling is too gradual to break the surface, yet it alters the small patterns passing over it. Reflections smooth slightly, and fine sand between the stones shifts in place. Attention descends from the brief contact of the water strider to the hidden movement modifying its ripples. The visible surface carries evidence of water arriving from beneath. At the downstream foot of the gravel shelf, water returned from beneath the creek bed. It rose through connected spaces between stones after passing below the sunlit riffle and through the cooler interior of the gravel. The return was distributed across several openings. No single spring disturbed the bed. Fine sand lifted slightly above one gap, settled, and lifted again when the upward movement increased. Nearby grains remained still where the passage was narrower. The upwelling water stayed close to the bottom at first. Its cooler condition gave it a different density from the surface water warmed along the exposed stones. The two layers met gradually as the current drew the lower water forward. Above the strongest opening, the surface remained smoother than the surrounding flow. Small ripples lost part of their curvature as they crossed it, then recovered downstream where the returning water had become more fully mixed. A thin root extended from the bank into the gravel shelf. Water passed around it from below and along it from upstream. The root altered both movements slightly without closing either path. The gravel retained evidence of earlier flows. Larger stones formed the stable frame of the shelf. Coarse sand occupied protected hollows. Finer material remained where the current had slowed enough to allow it to settle. Water entering the bed upstream had traveled through this arrangement before returning here. During that passage it exchanged heat with stone, moved through darkness, and reentered the open channel without a visible boundary. The creek received the returning water as part of its existing direction. Surface warmth was moderated. Cooler depth was distributed forward. Neither condition remained unchanged. Renewal occurred through circulation rather than replacement. As the upwelling joins the warmed channel, a broad canopy shadow begins moving across the reach. The sun has risen higher, changing the angle at which alder and sycamore branches interrupt the light. Stones that received direct warmth in Anchor 73 pass gradually into shade. The transition does not restore the creek to its earlier morning condition. The stones retain heat. The channel carries water already altered by sunlight and by the cooler return from beneath the gravel. Moss remains saturated where capillary movement has continued, and the alder roots still preserve their open passages. Shade arrives across a system that has not stopped changing. Its recurrence is therefore not reversal. It becomes another condition entering the accumulated state of the creek. A broad shadow moved from the western bank across the channel. Its edge was softened by many leaves, each contributing a small interval of shade that shifted with the upper air. The exposed stones entered darkness one at a time. Their surfaces no longer reflected direct sunlight, but they retained warmth gathered during the previous interval. Thin water crossing them cooled gradually rather than immediately. At the foot of the gravel shelf, the returning underflow remained cooler. The difference between it and the shaded surface narrowed as the current carried both downstream. Surface shimmer diminished, and the water became more transparent above the shallower stones. The moss margin remained dark beneath the bank. Capillary moisture continued moving through fine soil and root spaces without responding visibly to the change in light. The upper moss tips, briefly brightened earlier, returned to shade while retaining a slightly lighter tone where partial drying had occurred. Alder root openings appeared more clearly after the strongest reflection left the surface. Water could be seen passing through the lowest arch, carrying a narrow line of light from an opening farther upstream. The water strider remained beneath the sedges. One small surface depression appeared near the bank, shifted, and disappeared into the shadow. Nothing returned to its original state. The warmed stones were not the stones of first gray. The shaded water contained the cool return from beneath the riffle. The moss had exchanged moisture with the morning air. The root waterline had lowered by a nearly imperceptible amount. Shade crossed these accumulated changes and joined them. The creek remained continuous not because conditions repeated exactly, but because each new condition entered what previous movement had preserved.
