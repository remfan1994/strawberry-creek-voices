The returning shade of midmorning moves downstream until the open channel approaches a place where another kind of edge begins to shape the creek. Root and gravel still define most of the margin. Water still receives sky directly. Yet farther ahead, the banks no longer widen according only to soil, vegetation, and flow. The change begins beside the water rather than over it. Ground is held more firmly. The margin becomes straighter. Space that had followed the irregular line of the creek begins to answer to another geometry. The current reaches this alteration without changing its origin or direction. It carries the same cool return from beneath the gravel, the same moisture gathered from the banks, and the same morning light that has followed it through the open corridor. What changes first is not the water. It is the amount of room being left around it. The creek remained open where the morning light crossed it. Water moved between banks shaped by root, sediment, stone, and repeated changes in flow. Sedges leaned over one side. On the other, a shallow gravel margin held moisture between its darker stones. Downstream, the line of the bank became straighter. At first the difference was small enough to appear only as a change in proportion. The creek continued at nearly the same width, but the ground beside it no longer followed every shallow curve. The margin held to a firmer line while the current continued making smaller adjustments within it. Light still reached the entire surface. Reflections of leaves crossed the water without interruption. The creek bed remained visible through the shallow sections, and cooler water still emerged between stones where the underflow returned. Nothing in the current announced that the space ahead would become different. Water entered the straighter reach carrying all the relations accumulated upstream. Moisture from the moss margin remained within it. The gravel had cooled part of it. Sunlight had warmed another part. Fine sediment continued to settle wherever velocity allowed. The creek was not waiting to become something else. It was already complete in movement. The altered banks appeared around that completeness. The straighter margin continues downstream. Material placed beside the channel occupies spaces where the bank had previously changed gradually with moisture and root growth. The creek remains open overhead, but its lateral freedom decreases. Water meeting the tightened edge turns back toward the center sooner. Small eddies form where irregular stone meets the new boundary, then flatten as the current enters the narrower passage. The first transformation is therefore not disappearance. It is compression. The creek can still be seen from above. It still receives rain, light, leaves, and moving air directly. But the relation between water and surrounding ground has begun to change from a broad exchange into a guided passage. The banks drew closer around the creek. Where soil had once sloped gradually toward the water, the margin now descended more directly. Roots remained in the older ground nearby, but fewer reached into the newly defined passage. The current moved beside a surface shaped to hold its line. Water responded without stopping. Along the straighter edge, the faster portion of the current shifted toward the center. A narrow zone of slower water remained against the boundary, carrying fine sediment downstream in intermittent clouds too slight to obscure the stones below. The channel had less room to adjust laterally. When the current encountered a shallow rise in the bed, it could no longer spread as widely toward the bank. More of the movement passed forward. The difference continued beneath the surface. Groundwater approaching from the side still found openings into the channel, but the routes were fewer and more concentrated. Moisture that once reached the creek through a broad porous margin now emerged at particular seams and lower gaps. Above, the sky remained open. Air moved along the water. Light reached the full width of the channel. A falling droplet could still enter from a leaf without passing any intervening structure. The creek had been constrained without yet being concealed. It carried the new pressure as another physical condition, preserving movement inside a space that had become less willing to move with it. As the narrowed channel continues, the open sky above it begins to contract. The first overhead structure does not cover a long reach. It crosses from one side to the other, joining the firmer margins already established beside the water. Morning remains visible beyond both ends, but beneath the crossing the creek enters a different acoustic and thermal space. The surface darkens before the current changes speed. Reflections of canopy give way to the underside of the structure. Sound that had dispersed into open air returns from the closer surfaces above and beside the channel. For the first time in the sequence, the creek passes beneath ground that continues across it. The crossing placed a roof above the creek. Daylight remained ahead and behind, but directly overhead the open sky ended. The surface changed immediately. Reflected leaves disappeared, replaced by a darker field broken only where light entered from the two openings. The current continued beneath. Sound became more contained. Water moving over a shallow stone produced a small note that returned from the overhead surface and sidewalls before joining the broader sound of flow. What had been easy to locate in the open channel became layered within the enclosed space. The air was cooler beneath the crossing. Stone and damp surfaces retained the night's temperature beyond the time when the exposed bank had begun to warm. Moisture gathered in the deeper seams and remained there after nearby open surfaces dried. Near the entrance, sunlight reached a short distance beneath the roof. It illuminated the upper edge of one submerged stone, then weakened before reaching the center of the covered passage. The current crossed that boundary without hesitation. For a few moments of travel, the creek had no sky directly above it. Then the downstream opening widened, reflections returned, and the water emerged again into morning. The crossing was short enough for both openings to remain part of the same view. Yet something fundamental had become possible. The creek could continue where the sky did not. Beyond the first crossing, open water returns, but the new relation does not remain isolated. The enclosed distance can be lengthened. The supported ground above can extend farther downstream. A crossing can become a covered reach. As enclosure increases, the downstream opening appears smaller from within. Light no longer fills the passage from both directions with equal strength. The creek begins spending more of its course beneath a surface whose purpose belongs to the world above it. Water still follows slope. Gravity has not changed. The watershed has not stopped arriving. Seepage still enters where openings permit it. The physical necessity of flow continues while visual access to that flow decreases. The next threshold is not simply another crossing. It is entry into sustained buried distance. The longer covered reach began where the open channel narrowed toward a dark rectangular opening. Water reached the threshold in full morning light. The surface carried sky until the final few centimeters, where the reflection compressed against shadow and disappeared beneath the roof. Inside, the current remained visible near the entrance. The bed continued at the same downward tendency. Shallow water crossed small deposits of sand and gravel that had moved into the passage during stronger flows. Damp marks along the lower walls recorded earlier changes in level. A short distance farther in, color diminished. The current became easier to follow by sound than by sight. Flow against the lower edge of the enclosure produced a continuous low resonance. Small irregularities in the bed introduced quieter variations within it. Water entering through a seam along one side joined the creek without receiving daylight. The seep was clear and cool. It had traveled through the surrounding ground and found the enclosed channel just as groundwater had found the open creek upstream. Above the roof, another surface could continue without opening for the water beneath it. Below, the creek carried the watershed forward. The distance to open sky increased behind the current. The entrance became a bright shape at the end of the enclosed reach, then a smaller one. The creek did not vanish at the boundary. Visibility withdrew from it. Within the longer covered reach, the bright entrance recedes until it no longer illuminates the bed. The creek now moves beneath a continuous layer of built ground. Above, weight is transferred across the structure and into the surrounding earth. Below, water continues through the space preserved for it. The two systems occupy the same horizontal route without sharing the same visible world. Rain falling on the surface above may enter drains and return to the creek farther along. Groundwater reaches seams from the sides. Upstream flow arrives continuously from the open watershed. The buried channel is not isolated from the city above it. It has become one of the ways the city and watershed remain physically connected. The creek moved beneath ground that no longer opened above it. The overhead structure carried the weight of the surface across the channel. The water below carried something different: runoff from the hills, seepage from saturated ground, and the continuing discharge of the watershed toward lower elevation. Neither movement could be understood entirely from the other surface. Above, the ground could appear continuous. Below, the creek remained directional. The enclosed air changed slowly in temperature. Without direct sunlight, damp surfaces retained coolness. Moisture condensed in small areas where warmer air entered and met the colder interior. Drops gathered, released, and returned to the current. Fine sediment accumulated along lower edges where velocity decreased. During stronger flow it would move again. The passage therefore retained its own patterns of deposition and removal even without exposed banks. At one seam, groundwater entered steadily enough to darken the surrounding surface. It traveled downward and joined the creek in a thin line. The distinction between seepage and channel water lasted only until the next shallow disturbance mixed them. The creek had lost canopy, reeds, and direct sky within this reach. It had not lost gravity, water, direction, or connection to the watershed. Its circumstances had changed profoundly. Its continuity had not. Above, the built surface gave little indication of the moving water beneath it. The farther the buried current travels, the less the surface above needs to acknowledge its exact position. A person crossing the ground overhead can move from one side of the former open channel to the other without descending a bank or stepping across water. Buildings, paths, and streets can align themselves according to their own geometry because the creek has been given another geometry below. This is where enclosure begins to alter memory. The creek remains physically active. Water continues moving beneath the same ground. Yet the visible cues that once required attention to it have been removed from ordinary passage. Burial becomes more than a roof over water. It becomes a separation between presence and awareness. Above the buried reach, the ground continued across the creek without revealing where one bank had once ended and the other begun. There was no visible waterline. No reflection showed the direction of flow. No sedge leaned over the current. No shifting brightness revealed shallow stone beneath the surface. The ordinary signs by which a creek could be recognized were absent. Below them, water continued westward. The current followed the enclosed grade through darkness, increasing when upstream water arrived and decreasing when the watershed released less. Seepage entered through the surrounding ground. Sediment moved through the lower passage according to velocity and depth. The creek remained responsive to conditions occurring far beyond the buried reach. Rain in the hills could change the water beneath this ground. Saturated soil upstream could strengthen the flow. A dry interval could reduce it. What happened where the creek remained visible continued to affect the place where it could no longer be seen. The surface above acquired its own continuity. Movement across it no longer required awareness of water underneath. The old relation between bank, channel, and crossing was no longer available to immediate perception. This was a different kind of darkness from night. Night had concealed the creek while leaving its place intact. Morning had restored visibility. Here, concealment had been built into the place itself. The creek continued beneath it, carrying the watershed through a landscape that could gradually learn to behave as though no creek were there.
